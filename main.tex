  % ---------- 1. New physics searches ----------
  \begin{block}{Searching for New Physics}

    Our physics expertise focuses on \textbf{well-motivated BSM extensions that may
    be undercovered at the LHC experiments} --- not because they are unlikely, but
    because they are hard to analyse. \textbf{Complex dark sectors} and
    \textbf{long-lived particles} are the prime examples. We respond to these
    analysis-related challenges by developing novel ML approaches: anomaly detection,
    transformers, foundation models, and more.

    \subhead{Dark sector mediators}

    \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.53\columnwidth}
      Potential \textbf{dark matter} candidates can be produced in the decays of
      heavy scalar particles. These dark matter particles can be
      \textbf{long-lived}, and decay far away from the primary proton collision
      point --- leaving behind \textbf{displaced decay vertices} rather than tracks
      that all point back to one spot.

      \vspace{0.4cm}
      Using new reconstruction techniques, we can identify these displaced vertices
      in the ATLAS \textbf{inner tracker} and in the \textbf{calorimeter}, decaying
      to pairs of electrons.
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.43\columnwidth}
      \centering
      \includegraphics[width=0.88\columnwidth]{figures/openhouse/dark_sector_feynman.png}

      \figcap{A heavy scalar $S$ decays to a pair of dark photons $Z_d$, each of
      which travels before decaying to an electron-positron pair.}
    \end{column}
    \end{columns}

  % ---------- 2. Trigger anomaly detection ----------
  % Laid out as text/figure pairs rather than full-width stacked figures, to
  % keep the section compact without dropping any of the content.
  \begin{block}{Anomaly Detection in the ATLAS Trigger}

    \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.73\columnwidth}
      The ATLAS \textbf{trigger} filters the LHC's \SI{40}{MHz} event rate down to a
      rate that can be stored to disk. That makes it the gatekeeper of the final
      dataset --- and therefore of all the physics that can be analysed offline.

      \vspace{0.3cm}
      Our group is \textbf{leading the effort} to introduce real-time anomaly
      detection algorithms into the ATLAS trigger, with the first implementation
      entering data-taking in 2025.
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.23\columnwidth}
      \centering
      \includegraphics[width=0.9\columnwidth]{figures/openhouse/atlas_logo.png}
    \end{column}
    \end{columns}

    \vspace{0.3cm}
    The events this trigger collects sit \emph{below existing trigger thresholds} ---
    a phase space \textbf{never before seen} by the LHC experiments --- opening up
    novel discovery potential for new physics.

    \vspace{0.4cm}
    \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.40\columnwidth}
      We use a \textbf{VAE-GAN} trained over collision events, modeled by the
      four-vectors of their constituent particles. The network learns to reconstruct
      ordinary events; events it reconstructs \emph{badly} are exactly the ones worth
      saving.
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.56\columnwidth}
      \centering
      \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/openhouse/vae_gan.png}

      \figcap{Encoder, decoder and discriminator: the reconstruction error becomes
      the anomaly score.}
    \end{column}
    \end{columns}

    \vspace{0.4cm}
    \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.42\columnwidth}
      To run these algorithms inside the tight latency and resource budget of the
      trigger, we employ \textbf{``fast ML''} techniques: compression (truncating
      low-weight nodes), quantization, and reducing the number of loss terms.

      \vspace{0.4cm}
      \textbf{Status:} the first ATLAS anomaly trigger is collecting data, and
      studies for Run 4 anomaly triggers are ongoing!
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.54\columnwidth}
      \centering
      \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/openhouse/anomaly_roc.png}

      \figcap{One trigger, many signals: a single model is sensitive to a wide range
      of new physics it was never trained on.}
    \end{column}
    \end{columns}

  \end{block}

  % ---------- 4. Edge ML ----------
  \begin{block}{Edge ML \& Smart Detectors}

    At-source (\textbf{``edge''}) deployment of AI/ML can address the challenges of
    future ultra-high data rate experiments --- but it demands novel solutions in
    microelectronics that deliver \textbf{reconfigurable digital logic} within
    stringent latency and power constraints. Rather than shipping every raw sample
    off the detector, a smart front-end sends only what matters.

    \vspace{0.4cm}
    \begin{figure}
      \centering
      \includegraphics[width=0.72\columnwidth]{figures/openhouse/drift_cell_ml.png}

      \figcap{Feature extraction at the edge: a whole pattern of ionization clusters
      in a drift cell is compressed by a small network into the few numbers that the
      rest of the experiment actually needs.}
    \end{figure}

    \vspace{0.3cm}
    We are developing \textbf{embedded FPGA (eFPGA)} technology, capable of deploying
    reconfigurable logic blocks directly onto \textbf{ASICs}. This combines the
    benefits of an FPGA with the flexibility and processing power required for
    on-detector implementation --- so the algorithm can be updated long after the
    chip is built.

    \vspace{0.4cm}
    \begin{figure}
      \centering
      \includegraphics[width=0.62\columnwidth]{figures/openhouse/efpga_board.png}

      \figcap{An eFPGA test chip on its carrier board (right: the bare die under a
      microscope) --- a reconfigurable ML engine small enough to live on the
      detector.}
    \end{figure}

    \vspace{0.3cm}
    In collaboration with engineers in SLAC's \textbf{Technology Innovation
    Directorate (TID)}, we explore eFPGA applications in advanced subsystems for
    future experiments such as \textbf{FCC-ee} --- including \textbf{drift chambers}
    and \textbf{dual-readout calorimeters} --- as well as for applications beyond
    particle physics, such as \textbf{photon science} and \textbf{quantum information
    science}.

  \end{block}


  % ---------- 5. Advancing ML on FPGAs ----------
  \begin{block}{Advancing ML \& Quantum ML on FPGAs}

    Also with TID, we develop novel methods to implement machine learning on
    \textbf{FPGAs}, targeting \textbf{ultra-fast inference} and \textbf{low power}
    for applications like online data acquisition and processing.

    \vspace{0.4cm}
    \begin{figure}
      \centering
      \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/openhouse/mlpmixer_to_fpga.png}

      \figcap{From architecture to arithmetic to silicon: a transformer-like network
      is rewritten as cheap add/shift operations, then mapped onto FPGA logic.}
    \end{figure}

    \subhead{Foundation models, distilled onto hardware}

    We train a \textbf{foundation model} targeted at detector \textbf{waveforms},
    then \textbf{compress and distil} it into a student network small enough to run
    on an FPGA. The large model learns the general shape of detector signals once;
    the distilled student carries that knowledge at a fraction of the cost.

    \vspace{0.4cm}
    The same recipe is being applied to large models targeting the wider
    \textbf{HEP-ex} domain, in pursuit of \textbf{real-time foundation models} ---
    state-of-the-art representations evaluated inside the latency budget of a trigger
    or a front-end ASIC.

    \vspace{0.4cm}

  \begin{block}{Who We Are}

    Our group pursues the application of the latest AI/ML techniques to the
    challenges of \textbf{data-taking} and \textbf{physics analysis} for the
    \textbf{ATLAS} experiment at the Large Hadron Collider, hosted at the
    \textbf{CERN} laboratory in Geneva, Switzerland. We are also involved in R\&D
    studies and \textbf{strategic planning for next-generation future colliders},
    such as the proposed \textbf{Future Circular Collider} at CERN.

    \vspace{0.4cm}
    We sit across SLAC's \textbf{Fundamental Physics} and \textbf{Technology
    Innovation} Directorates and work with \textbf{Stanford University}, spanning the
    full chain: from asking what new physics might look like, to training the network,
    to taping out the chip that runs it.
