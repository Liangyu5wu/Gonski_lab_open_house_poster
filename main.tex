  \begin{block}{What Are We Looking For?}

    \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.60\columnwidth}
      The \textbf{Standard Model} of particle physics describes every particle we
      have ever discovered --- and yet we know it is incomplete. It says nothing
      about dark matter, and cannot explain why the universe contains matter at
      all. Something \emph{new} must be out there.

      \vspace{0.4cm}
      At CERN's \textbf{Large Hadron Collider}, the \textbf{ATLAS} experiment
      smashes protons together 40 million times per second, hoping to make that
      new physics in the lab. The catch: we can only afford to record a tiny
      fraction of those collisions, and the decision about what to keep has to be
      made in \emph{microseconds}.

      \vspace{0.4cm}
      Our group attacks that problem from both ends --- the
      \textbf{algorithms} that decide what is interesting, and the
      \textbf{microelectronics} that run them right next to the detector.
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.36\columnwidth}
      \centering
      \includegraphics[width=0.72\columnwidth]{figures/openhouse/atlas_logo.png}

      \figcap{The ATLAS experiment at CERN}
    \end{column}
    \end{columns}

  \end{block}


  \begin{alertblock}{Our Big Idea}

    Instead of telling the detector \emph{exactly} what to look for, we teach it
    what ordinary collisions look like --- and then ask it to flag anything
    \textbf{surprising}. Doing this in real time, in a chip, means we can catch new
    physics we never thought to search for.

  \end{alertblock}


  \begin{block}{Anomaly Detection in the ATLAS Trigger}

    \lead{Looking for anything that isn't ``normal.''}

    Machine learning-based anomaly detection in offline ATLAS searches can enable
    broad sensitivity to \emph{any} kind of new physics that is not Standard
    Model-like. Pushing this capability down to the \textbf{trigger level} --- the
    ultra-low-latency system that decides which collisions to keep --- gives us the
    potential to write out never-before-seen events from the LHC.

    \vspace{0.4cm}
    We use a \textbf{VAE-GAN} trained over collision events, modeled by the
    four-vectors of their constituent particles. The network learns to reconstruct
    ordinary events; events it reconstructs \emph{badly} are exactly the ones worth
    saving.

    \vspace{0.5cm}
    \begin{figure}
      \centering
      \includegraphics[width=0.98\columnwidth]{figures/openhouse/vae_gan.png}

      \figcap{The VAE-GAN: an encoder compresses each event, a decoder rebuilds it,
      and a discriminator sharpens the result. Reconstruction error becomes the
      anomaly score.}
    \end{figure}

    \vspace{0.5cm}
    To run these algorithms inside the tight latency and resource budget of the
    ATLAS trigger, we employ \textbf{``fast ML''} techniques: compression
    (truncating low-weight nodes), quantization, and reducing the number of loss
    terms.

    \vspace{0.5cm}
    \begin{figure}
      \centering
      \includegraphics[width=0.88\columnwidth]{figures/openhouse/anomaly_roc.png}

      \figcap{One trigger, many signals: a single anomaly detection model is
      sensitive to a wide range of new physics models it was never trained on.}
    \end{figure}

    \vspace{0.4cm}
    \textbf{Status:} the first ATLAS anomaly trigger is on track for data-taking in
    2025, and studies for Run 4 anomaly triggers are ongoing!

  \end{block}


  \begin{block}{Searching for Dark Sector Mediators}

    \lead{Hunting particles that decay far from where they were born.}

    Potential \textbf{dark matter} candidates can be produced in the decays of heavy
    scalar particles. These dark matter particles can be \textbf{long-lived}, and
    decay far away from the primary proton collision point --- leaving behind
    \textbf{displaced decay vertices} rather than tracks that all point back to one
    spot.

    \vspace{0.5cm}
    \begin{figure}
      \centering
      \includegraphics[width=0.78\columnwidth]{figures/openhouse/dark_sector_feynman.png}

      \figcap{A heavy scalar $S$ decays to a pair of dark photons $Z_d$, each of
      which travels before decaying to an electron-positron pair.}
    \end{figure}

    \vspace{0.4cm}
    Using new reconstruction techniques, we can identify these displaced vertices in
    the ATLAS \textbf{inner tracker} and in the \textbf{calorimeter}, decaying to
    pairs of electrons. Anomaly detection is also used here, to look for signs of
    new physics decaying into electron or photon final states with unusual
    topologies.

  \end{block}

\end{column}

\begin{column}{\colwidth}

  \begin{block}{Edge ML \& Smart Detectors}

    \lead{Put the intelligence where the data is born.}

    Machine learning at the detector \textbf{``edge''} --- the source of the data ---
    can reduce the off-detector data rate and enable a far more efficient trigger and
    data acquisition system. Rather than shipping every raw sample off the detector,
    a smart front-end sends only what matters.

    \vspace{0.5cm}
    \begin{figure}
      \centering
      \includegraphics[width=0.85\columnwidth]{figures/openhouse/drift_cell_ml.png}

      \figcap{Feature extraction at the edge: a whole pattern of ionization clusters
      in a drift cell is compressed by a small network into the few numbers that the
      rest of the experiment actually needs.}
    \end{figure}

    \vspace{0.4cm}
    In collaboration with SLAC's \textbf{Technology Innovation Directorate (TID)}, we
    are exploring \textbf{eFPGAs} for ML-based signal processing, leveraging
    innovative data compression algorithms in advanced tracker and calorimeter
    concepts.

    \vspace{0.4cm}
    For example, detectors at next-generation colliders such as CERN's
    \textbf{Future Circular Collider} are looking at \textbf{drift chamber
    trackers}; we develop small compression models that perform key feature
    extraction for deployment directly in the front-end readout.

    \vspace{0.5cm}
    \begin{figure}
      \centering
      \includegraphics[width=0.85\columnwidth]{figures/openhouse/efpga_board.png}

      \figcap{An eFPGA test chip on its carrier board (right: the bare die under a
      microscope) --- a reconfigurable ML engine small enough to live on the
      detector.}
    \end{figure}

    \vspace{0.4cm}
    \textbf{Embedded field programmable gate arrays (eFPGAs)} allow the design of an
    ASIC that is usable at the detector front-end while still carrying a
    \textbf{reconfigurable} digital logic fabric --- so the algorithm can be updated
    long after the chip is built.

  \end{block}


  \begin{block}{Advancing ML on FPGAs \& MLP-Mixers}

    \lead{Making state-of-the-art architectures fit in real hardware.}

    Also with TID, we develop novel methods to implement machine learning on
    \textbf{FPGAs}, targeting \textbf{ultra-fast inference} and \textbf{low power}
    for applications like online data acquisition and processing.

    \vspace{0.5cm}
    \begin{figure}
      \centering
      \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/openhouse/mlpmixer_to_fpga.png}

      \figcap{From architecture to arithmetic to silicon: the MLP-Mixer is rewritten
      as cheap add/shift operations, then mapped onto FPGA logic.}
    \end{figure}

    \vspace{0.4cm}
    The \textbf{MLP-Mixer} architecture --- promising for resource-limited,
    transformer-like learning --- has been successfully implemented. This enables
    edge and real-time ML to \textbf{scale up} and take advantage of state-of-the-art
    ML techniques, rather than being stuck with only the smallest networks.

    \vspace{0.5cm}
    \begin{figure}
      \centering
      \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/openhouse/fpga_rack.png}

      \figcap{Larger models are split across multiple FPGAs in a single server rack,
      linked by high-speed streaming interfaces.}
    \end{figure}

  \end{block}


  \begin{block}{Why It Matters}

    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Discovery reach.} A trigger that recognizes ``weird'' instead of
      one specific signature keeps our options open for physics nobody has predicted.
      \item \textbf{Data volume.} Smart front-ends cut the data rate at the source,
      which is what makes the next generation of ultra-granular detectors affordable
      at all.
      \item \textbf{Beyond physics.} The same recipe --- tiny, quantized networks on
      radiation-tolerant reconfigurable chips with nanosecond latency --- shows up in
      medical imaging, accelerator controls, photon science, and autonomous systems.
    \end{itemize}

  \end{block}
